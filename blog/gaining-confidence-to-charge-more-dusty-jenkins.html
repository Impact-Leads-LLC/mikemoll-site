How to create a group coaching program people will pay for

Dusty Jenkins wanted to launch a group for dads through Valiant Voyages, but he felt he needed a full program built first. On this live coaching episode we worked out a simpler offer: an open monthly membership, a price that reads per call, a landing page that leads with the outcome, and the confidence to charge for it.

How do you create a group coaching program people will pay for?

Sell support and access before you build a curriculum. People join a group to stay accountable and to be around others who get them. Set one monthly price with no end date, run group calls with breakout rooms, and let members stay as long as they like. Talk to early buyers first, then write the page from their words.

What makes people pay for a group coaching program?

People pay for access, support and a group that holds them to their word. Dusty Jenkins came on the show feeling stuck. He didn't have a six-week coaching program fully built for his dads group, Valiant Voyages. The curriculum matters far less than he thought. I have clients who pay me thousands of dollars a month because they like the accountability of talking to me. They also like that I often have a fix for their problem that they haven't thought of yet.

I compared it to joining a baseball team. A team makes you exercise and it makes you see people. On a team, you go to the gym because you can't let the others down. People join a coaching group for the same reason: to hold themselves to something and to have people around who support them.

Dusty wants to reach fathers who feel distracted and want to be more intentional with their families. The founder of a company I work with that runs addiction recovery intensives told me community is one of the biggest parts of recovery. I think dads need that kind of place too, because most of them won't talk about this stuff with their co-workers.

How should you structure a group coaching program?

Set it up as an open membership with a monthly price, and let people stay for as long as they want support. I told Dusty I wouldn't build it as a six-week or one-year course with a set curriculum. Let the group grow into what its members need. You could have a hundred dads in one group, with a share each week, a group exercise and breakout rooms. That works with a big group, and you never have to kick anyone out when a program ends.

I took the format from a men's group I was in for a few months. Everyone got five minutes to share. When the group got too big, the host split us into breakout rooms of up to four people. Each person had three minutes for something going well and three minutes for something they were stuck on. At the end, you got three minutes of feedback from the others in your circle. I found those small, timed rooms amazing because they worked like little masterminds, and you met different people with different ideas.

The host picked a topic each week, and there was an accountability piece too. You set a goal for the month, like going to the gym three days a week. If you missed it, you owed 200 burpees and had to send the video.

How much should you charge for group coaching?

For Dusty's group, I suggested something like $150 a month, for as long as a member wants support. Break the price down per call, and it looks small next to what members get. I pointed out to Dusty that $150 a month for two calls comes to $75 a call. I told him that is cheaper than therapy, and probably better than therapy.

Most of the guys I went to high school with are married with two kids. They will never spend four hours a week on therapy work like I do. Picture one call on a Wednesday night from 7:00 to 8:30, in a safe place to unload and get advice. I told Dusty that for $75 a call, that's nothing.

Dusty shared his own example: he is in a coaching program with two 90-minute sessions a month, one where the coach teaches and one for open questions. The men's group I was in cost me $80 a month for one call every other Sunday and a WhatsApp group. If it had been run better, with the type of people I wanted to be around, I said I probably would have paid more.

Should a group coaching program start online or in person?

Start online, and add in-person meetups once members ask for them. Dusty's first plan was local pop-up dinner workshops and overnight trips for dads. He already travels to retreats for work, and he said each trip means about five days away from his family. Dusty saw that running dinners once or twice a month wouldn't pay the bills if this was his full-time work.

A one-day event in one city is hard to sell when your audience is small. When you host an online group, members start to ask if they can meet up. Say you have 87 dads spread across the US. You can plan a retreat in Atlanta for the ones who want to come.

Dusty took the idea further. In his plan, a local dinner with eight seats becomes a perk of the membership. He can pop up wherever members are, like a Colorado crew in June. I added that he can see where people sign up from and hold the meetup in the middle.

Finding your first membersDo your buyers spend time in Facebook groups? My episode with Megan Van Noy covers how to market a coaching offer there.

What should a coaching landing page say above the fold?

Above the fold, a visitor should see four things: a headline with the outcome, a sub headline that tells them they're in the right place, a call to action, and proof. Dusty's page had one of the four. His headline asked dads if they felt like a distracted father, then said "Let's be intentional together." I would lead with the outcome the dad wants. Something like "We help fathers become more intentional," with a sub headline about turning distracted, distant dads into the men they want to be.

Opening on the negative pushes on a dad who may already feel low about himself as a father. Many coaches lead with the pain. I told Dusty that this is my own opinion, and that I've been moving toward leading with the dream.

For proof, a one-sentence testimonial works, or a line like "over 20 five-star reviews on Google." Then add a button with the next step.

What goes below the fold on a coaching landing page?

Right below the fold, describe the product and how members get the outcome. For Dusty, that means saying plainly that this is an online support group where Christian dads come together. Put the proof and the program first, and save your own story for further down. I told Dusty to move everything about himself into one section near the end, with a "Hi, I'm Dusty" heading. That includes the story about his nine-year-old daughter.

Before the program, describe the challenge dads share. They're busy, they have less time to see friends, and they have no one to lean on. Then show what the program is and what members get from it.

When you write those benefits, start with what the member gets and follow with how it happens. I gave Dusty an example. For "two group coaching calls every month," write "feel supported by other dads in our bi-weekly group calls." For "WhatsApp group," write "stay in touch and build community through our group WhatsApp." For the third, write: get a fresh set of eyes and ears on the challenges you face as a parent, from dads like you.

What should your call to action be when you're starting out?

Early on, make the call to action a call with you, and get on the phone with as many people as you can. Dusty asked whether it should be a 15-minute call or a form to see if the group is right for them. Those first calls are your customer research. You don't know yet what makes someone want a group like this.

Ask them to tell you about themselves and why they think a group like this would help. Ask what they'd most want to take away, and what would be a win if they joined.

Use what they tell you as the words on the landing page. For Dusty, the headline and the benefits would come from what dads told him they want. The rest of his page, like the "Dad, can you play?" story about pushing kids off, can stay below that.

How do you get the confidence to charge more for coaching?

Picture yourself as the buyer, and ask if you would pay for it. I know Dusty well, and he overthinks. Everyone on the planet has some impostor syndrome (the feeling of being a fraud), but he lets it steer his decisions. Dusty is his own customer, so if he would pay for the group, other dads like him will too. I asked him to imagine a group of supportive dads where he could share his wins and what he thinks he's screwing up. I think Dusty would pay $150 a month for two calls with them.

Dusty said his overthinking tells him to list every benefit so the money seems to stretch. I charge a lot more now, and I do fewer things for each client. I used to hand clients a list of 17 things we'd tackle together. Now I tell them that to fix the problem they care about, we go back to the root and fix three things. When they want that fixed, I tell them it's 15 grand. They say it sounds good, because the result will make them far more money than that.

For Dusty's group, I said the offer is the support of other dads and getting a little better as a father every month, for $150 a month.

Raising your priceHas your offer grown a long list of deliverables? How to raise your prices without adding more work covers which ones to cut as the price goes up.

What can you do this week?

  1. Write down what you would want from your group if you were the buyer, and how much a month it would be worth to you.
  2. Set one monthly price with no end date, and work out what it comes to per call.
  3. Get on calls with as many people who might join as you can, and ask what a win would be for them.
  4. Change your headline to the outcome, and put a sub headline, proof and a button at the top of the page.
  5. Turn each feature on your page into a benefit, with the feature after it.

If you're not sure yet who the group is for, my episode with Susie Ippolito covers how to pick your ideal client.
